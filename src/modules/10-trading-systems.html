BEFORE YOU READ THIS

This is an advanced, optional module. Most people building long-term wealth never need any of it — a simple, low-cost index fund held for years (Module 5) does the job. Read this to understand what active trading involves, not as a nudge to start. If anything here feels exciting, please finish section 7 first.

1. What a trading system is

A trading system is just a fixed set of written rules that decides when to buy and when to sell. The whole point is that the decision comes from the plan, not from how you happen to feel in the moment.

Why does that matter? Because in live markets, emotion is the enemy. When a stock is rocketing up, you feel greed and want to pile in. When it's crashing, you feel fear and want to run. A system replaces "what should I do right now?" — a question your panicking brain answers badly — with "what do my rules say?" — a question you decided calmly, in advance.

Eg: A very simple rule-based system might say: "Buy when the price rises above its average of the last 50 days; sell when it falls back below that average." Whether or not that particular rule is any good, notice the key feature — there's no guessing. The rule is the same on a scary day as on a calm one.

So a trading system is not a magic money machine. It is a discipline tool: a way to make your future self behave predictably, even when markets are doing their best to scare or tempt you.

2. The core parts

Most trading systems, however simple or fancy, are built from the same few building blocks. If even one is missing, the system has a hole in it. Here are the core parts:

Part What it decides
Entry rule The exact condition that tells you when to buy.
Exit rule When to sell to take a profit once a trade has gone your way.
Stop-loss When to sell to cut a loss if the trade goes against you.
Position sizing How much money to put into each individual trade.
Target The price you're aiming for — the profit level that would make the trade a success.

Beginners obsess over the entry rule — the "secret" of when to buy. But experienced traders will tell you the entry is the least important part. The rules that decide how much you risk and when you cut losses (the next section) are what actually keep you in the game.

3. Stop-loss & position sizing — the heart of survival

If you remember only one section from this whole module, make it this one. Survival in trading is not about being right often. It's about making sure that being wrong never wipes you out. Two rules do the heavy lifting.

Position sizing: never risk too much on one trade. A common rule of thumb is to risk no more than a small slice — say 1% to 2% — of your total capital on any single trade. "Risk" here means the amount you'd lose if that trade hit its stop-loss, not the amount you invest.

Eg: Suppose your trading capital is ₹1,00,000 and you cap risk at 2%. That means you're willing to lose at most ₹2,000 on a single trade. Even if you got ten trades wrong in a row — a horrible streak — you'd be down ₹20,000, bruised but still standing with ₹80,000. Without such a rule, one bad trade can take a huge bite out of your money.

Stop-loss: decide your exit before you enter. A stop-loss is a pre-set price at which you will sell to cap the damage when a trade goes the wrong way. You set it the moment you buy — calmly — so that if the price falls to that level, you're already out. It removes the agonising "should I hold and hope?" question that ruins so many traders.

THE MATH IS BRUTAL ON BIG LOSSES

Losses hurt more than equal-sized gains help. If you lose 50% of your money, you then need a 100% gain just to get back to where you started. This is exactly why protecting your capital matters more than chasing the next big win — a wiped-out account can't trade at all.

The mindset shift is this: a good trader's first question is never "how much could I make?" It's "how much could I lose, and can I survive it?" Defence comes before offence.

4. Backtesting

Backtesting means taking your set of rules and testing them against past market data — pretending you had traded them over, say, the last ten years — to see how they would have performed, all before you risk a single real rupee.

That sounds sensible, and it is. But backtesting is full of traps that fool beginners into false confidence:

  • Overfitting. This is the big one. If you keep tweaking your rules until they look perfect on past data, you've often just memorised that specific history — not discovered something that works. Eg: "Buy only on rainy Tuesdays in stocks starting with T" might score beautifully on old data by pure coincidence, yet mean nothing for the future. A system tuned too tightly to the past tends to fall apart in the present.
  • Ignoring costs. A backtest that forgets brokerage and transaction costs (Module 6) flatters itself. Real trades cost money every time you buy and sell, and those small fees add up fast for an active trader — sometimes turning a "winning" system into a losing one.
  • Ignoring slippage. Slippage is the gap between the price you hoped to trade at and the price you actually got, because the market moved in the split second your order went through. Backtests assume perfect prices; reality is messier.

THE HONEST LIMIT

Even a careful, cost-aware backtest only tells you what would have happened. The past does not guarantee the future. Markets change, conditions shift, and a rule that shone for a decade can quietly stop working. Treat a good backtest as a reason not to be reckless — never as a promise.

5. Types of systems

You'll hear traders talk about many "strategies," but most fall into two broad families that pull in opposite directions:

  • Trend-following. The idea is to ride a move that's already underway — buy something that is rising, on the bet that it keeps rising for a while. "The trend is your friend," as the old saying goes.
  • Mean-reversion. The opposite idea: buy the dips, betting that a price which has fallen unusually far will bounce back toward its usual level.

Notice they contradict each other — one buys strength, the other buys weakness. That's the point: there is no single "holy grail" system that always works. Each approach wins in some market conditions and loses in others, and no rulebook escapes that.

6. Discipline & journaling

Here's the uncomfortable truth about trading systems: a system only works if you actually follow it. The best rules in the world are useless the moment you override them because "this time feels different." Most system failures aren't failures of the rules — they're failures of the person breaking them.

Two habits help you stay honest:

  1. Keep a trade journal. Write down every trade: what your rule told you to do, what you actually did, why, and how it turned out. Over time this reveals your real patterns — including the costly moments when emotion took over.
  2. Review it regularly. A journal you never re-read is just a diary. Going back over it is how you spot whether you're truly following your system or quietly sabotaging it.

And this points to the hardest part of all: the real battle is psychology, not strategy. Fear, greed, impatience and the urge to "get even" after a loss wreck more traders than bad rules ever do. This is exactly the territory we covered in Module 4 on risk and mindset — and it matters even more here, where money is on the line every single day.

TIP

If you can't stick to a boring, simple set of rules in calm conditions, you almost certainly won't manage it when real money is swinging and your heart is pounding. Honest self-knowledge is worth more than any clever indicator.

7. A realistic look

Now the part the rest of the internet often hides from you. We've saved it for last on purpose, because it's the most important thing in this module.

THE ODDS ARE GENUINELY AGAINST ACTIVE TRADING

Trading costs, taxes (Module 6) and raw emotion sink the great majority of trading systems over time. And it's not just opinion: study after study finds that most active traders underperform a simple, low-cost index fund held patiently for the long term — many lose money outright once costs are counted. The people selling courses, tips and "guaranteed" strategies are usually making their money from you, not from trading.

So where does that leave a beginner? With grounded expectations:

  • Active trading is hard, costly and stressful, and being smart or hard-working does not guarantee success at it.
  • For almost everyone, long-term investing in low-cost funds is the calmer, better-proven path — and it needs none of the machinery in this module.
  • If you still choose to try active trading, treat every rupee of trading money as money you can genuinely afford to lose. Never use rent, emergency savings, borrowed money, or anything you'll need soon.

There's no shame in deciding trading isn't for you. Honestly, walking away from it is the choice that quietly serves most people best.
