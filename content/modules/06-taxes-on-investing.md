# Module 6 · Taxes on Investing

When your investments make money, a part of that profit is taxable — here's how it works in plain English.

## 1. Why this matters

Here is a simple truth: when you make money from investing, the government wants a small share of it as **tax**. That's normal — it's the same idea as paying tax on your salary.

The good news is that it's much simpler than it looks. For most beginners, only a few rules really matter, and your broker hands you most of the numbers you need. You don't have to calculate everything by hand.

One thing to keep in mind throughout this module: **tax rules change**. The government updates them almost every year in the annual **Budget**. So treat this page as "the current basics" — enough to understand what's going on — and always check the latest rules before you actually file.

> [!NOTE] A quick note on words
>
> A **financial year** (FY) in India runs from 1 April to 31 March, not January to December. So "this year's gains" means gains made within that April-to-March window. The form you use to declare your income to the tax department is called an **ITR** (Income Tax Return).

## 2. The three kinds of tax

Before we zoom in on investing, it helps to see the bigger picture. In India there are **three** kinds of tax worth knowing about — and, happily, the gains you make from investing are taxed the most *gently* of the three.

| Kind of tax | Charged on | Roughly how much |
|---|---|---|
| **Income tax** | Your regular earnings — salary, interest (from an FD or savings account), rent, pension | Slab rates, up to about **30%** — the highest |
| **Corporate tax** | The profits a company makes | Around **25%** for most domestic companies |
| **Capital gains tax** | The profit you make by investing | The **lowest** — e.g. long-term equity gains are taxed at just 12.5% (the next sections break this down) |

**Income tax** is the one most people already know. It's charged on your regular earnings — your **salary**, the **interest** from a fixed deposit or savings account, any **rent** you receive, a pension, and so on. It uses **slab rates**: the more you earn, the higher the rate on the upper portions of your income, rising to about 30%. Of the three, income is taxed the **highest**.

**Corporate tax** is what *companies* pay on their profits — around 25% for most domestic companies. You won't pay this one yourself as an individual investor, but it's useful to know it sits in the middle.

**Capital gains tax** is what this module is really about: the tax on the profit you make when an *investment* you own rises in value and you sell it. As the table hints, this is taxed the most kindly of the three — and the rest of this module unpacks exactly how.

> [!NOTE] Why investing is taxed so gently
>
> A simple way to make sense of the order is to think about **risk**. Earning a salary or FD interest is fairly **low-risk**, so the government taxes it the most. Running a business carries real risk, so companies get a lower rate to **encourage** that risk-taking. And investing puts your money **at risk** — so gains from it are taxed the least, as a reward for taking that risk, especially over the long term. In short: **lower risk → higher tax; higher risk → lower tax**. (That's a handy way to remember it, not an official rulebook.)

One thing worth clearing up early: if you earn **both** a salary and some investing profit, each part is taxed under **its own rule** — they don't merge into a single rate. Your salary is taxed at income-tax slab rates; your investing gains are taxed under the capital-gains rules we're about to cover. So let's start with what a capital gain actually is.

## 3. What are capital gains?

A **capital gain** is simply the profit you make when you **sell** something for more than you paid for it. If you buy shares for ₹50,000 and later sell them for ₹70,000, your capital gain is ₹20,000.

Two important points:

- The gain is only counted **when you sell**. If your shares go up in value but you keep holding them, there's no tax yet — the profit is only "on paper."
- If you sell for *less* than you paid, that's a **capital loss**, not a gain — and there's no tax on a loss (in fact, losses can be useful, as we'll see in section 7).

For shares and equity mutual funds, the tax depends on **how long you held** the investment before selling. This is called the **holding period**, and it splits your gains into two buckets:

- **Short-term:** you held the investment for **12 months or less**.
- **Long-term:** you held it for **more than 12 months** (i.e. a year and a day or longer).

So the same ₹20,000 profit can be taxed differently depending on whether you sold after, say, 8 months (short-term) or after 14 months (long-term). Long-term is treated more kindly, as the next section shows.

> [!TIP]
>
> The 12-month line only applies to **listed shares and equity mutual funds** (the kind of investing this site covers). Other assets like property or gold use different holding periods — but those are beyond what a beginner needs here.

## 4. The rates for equity

For listed shares and equity mutual funds, here are the current rates. The short-term rate is a flat percentage; the long-term rate comes with a generous yearly exemption.

| Type of gain | Holding period | Tax rate |
|---|---|---|
| **Short-Term Capital Gain (STCG)** | 12 months or less | 20% on the whole gain |
| **Long-Term Capital Gain (LTCG)** | More than 12 months | 12.5% — but the first ₹1.25 lakh of LTCG each financial year is tax-free |

That ₹1.25 lakh exemption is worth understanding properly. Each financial year, you can make up to **₹1,25,000 of long-term gains completely tax-free**. Only the part *above* ₹1.25 lakh gets taxed at 12.5%.

Eg: Suppose in one financial year your long-term capital gains add up to **₹2,00,000**.

1. The first **₹1,25,000** is exempt — no tax on it at all.
2. That leaves **₹75,000** (₹2,00,000 − ₹1,25,000) as the taxable part.
3. Tax = 12.5% of ₹75,000 = **₹9,375**.

![Each financial year the first ₹1.25 lakh of long-term gains is completely tax-free — only the slice above it is taxed, here at 12.5%.](../assets/img/diagrams/ltcg-exemption-split.svg)

So on a ₹2 lakh long-term profit, you'd pay only ₹9,375 in LTCG tax. Short-term gains don't get this exemption — they're taxed at 20% from the very first rupee.

> [!NOTE] Why this encourages patience
>
> Notice how the system rewards holding longer: long-term gains are taxed at a lower rate (12.5% vs 20%) *and* get a big tax-free cushion. This is one quiet reason long-term investing is often gentler on your returns.

## 5. Equity vs debt funds

Not all mutual funds are taxed the same way. The rates above apply to **equity** funds (which mainly invest in shares). **Debt** funds (which invest in bonds and similar) follow a different, simpler-but-stricter rule.

| Investment | How gains are taxed |
|---|---|
| **Equity funds & listed shares** | STCG at 20%; LTCG at 12.5% with the ₹1.25 lakh yearly exemption (as in section 4) |
| **Debt funds** (bought on or after 1 April 2023) | The gain is added to your income and taxed at your normal income-tax **slab rate** — no matter how long you held it. There is *no* special long-term benefit. |

What does "**slab rate**" mean? India taxes income in slabs — the more you earn, the higher the rate on the upper portions. So if you're in, say, the 20% slab, your debt-fund gains are effectively taxed at 20% along with the rest of your income. For someone in a higher slab, the rate is higher.

The key takeaway: **debt funds bought from 1 April 2023 onwards lost the lower long-term rate**. Holding a debt fund for years no longer gives you a tax discount — the gain is simply added to your income whenever you sell.

> [!TIP]
>
> Not sure whether a fund is "equity" or "debt"? As a rough rule, a fund is treated as equity for tax if it keeps at least 65% of its money in Indian shares. The fund's own information document states its category — and your broker's statement usually labels the gains correctly too.

## 6. How dividends are taxed

A **dividend** is a share of profit that a company pays out to its shareholders, usually as cash. It's nice to receive — but it's taxable.

Dividends are added to your income and taxed at your **slab rate**, just like your salary. There's no special low rate for dividends.

There's also a small mechanic called **TDS** (Tax Deducted at Source). If the dividends you receive from a single company cross **₹5,000 in a financial year**, that company will deduct **10% TDS** before paying you, and send that money to the tax department on your behalf.

Eg: A company declares ₹6,000 in dividends for you in a year. Since that's above ₹5,000, it deducts 10% (₹600) as TDS and pays you ₹5,400. The ₹600 isn't lost — it's a prepayment of your tax.

When you file your ITR, you can **claim credit** for that TDS — meaning it's adjusted against your total tax. If too much was deducted, you may even get a refund. So the TDS is not an extra tax; it's just collected early.

> [!NOTE] Good to know
>
> Because dividends are taxed at your slab rate, they aren't automatically "tax-free income." Always include them when you file, even the small ones — the tax department already has a record of them.

## 7. Other bits: STT & loss harvesting

Two small things round out the picture.

**STT (Securities Transaction Tax).** This is a tiny tax charged automatically on every buy and sell of shares on the stock exchange. You don't pay it separately or file anything for it — it's deducted as part of the transaction itself, baked into the cost. It's usually a very small amount, so most beginners barely notice it, but it's good to know it exists.

**Tax-loss harvesting.** In one line: this is the practice of deliberately **selling a losing investment to book the loss**, so that loss can offset (cancel out) some of your taxable gains and reduce your tax. It's a more advanced idea — just know the term exists and that losses aren't entirely wasted.

> [!TIP]
>
> You don't need to chase tax-loss harvesting as a beginner. The main thing to remember is the simple principle behind it: capital *losses* can be set against capital *gains*, lowering the gains you're taxed on.

## 8. Filing basics

Once a year, you declare your income and gains to the tax department by filing your **ITR**. Here's the gentle version of what's involved for an investor.

- Your **broker provides a statement** — often called a **capital-gains statement** or **P&L (profit and loss) statement**. It lists what you bought and sold and the gains for the year, already split into short-term and long-term. This does most of the hard work for you.
- For most investors, capital gains are reported in the form called **ITR-2**.
- If you also trade **F&O (futures & options) as a business**, you'd typically use **ITR-3** instead, because that's treated as business income rather than simple investing.
- **Keep your statements safe.** Save the broker statements and any TDS records for each year — they're your proof if the numbers are ever questioned.

You don't have to memorise form names. The point is simply: your broker gives you the figures, you put them in the right return once a year, and you keep the paperwork. Many people take help from a CA (Chartered Accountant) for this, especially in the early years.

> [!WARNING]
>
> Tax rules change with every annual Budget. The figures here are the **current basics** (rules as of 2026, applicable since 23 July 2024) — they are **not personal advice**. Before you file, confirm the latest rules with a qualified CA or official sources such as the income tax department's website, **incometax.gov.in**.
