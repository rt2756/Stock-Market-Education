# Module 2 · Analysing Stocks

How do people decide what to buy? There are two main approaches. You don't need to master either — just understand what they are.

## 1. Two ways to study a stock {#two-ways}

Suppose you want to buy a used car. You could check two very different things:

- **Is the car itself good?** Engine, service history, mileage, brand. → This is like **fundamental analysis**.
- **Is the price moving up or down lately, and why?** What are buyers willing to pay this week? → This is like **technical analysis**.

![Two very different lenses on the same share: one asks "is the business any good?", the other "which way is the price moving?"](../assets/img/diagrams/02-analysing-stocks-1.svg)

Both look at the same car, but answer different questions. Investors do the same with companies.

## 2. Fundamental analysis — is the business good? {#fundamental}

Fundamental analysis asks the most sensible question of all: *is this a good company, and is its share fairly priced?* You study the actual business — what it sells, whether it makes a profit, whether it has too much loan, and whether it's growing.

Most of this comes from documents companies must publish every few months, called **financial statements**. The three you'll hear about:

| Statement | Answers the question… |
|---|---|
| **Income statement** (P&L) | Did the company make a profit? (Money in, money out.) |
| **Balance sheet** | What does it own, and what does it owe? (Assets vs debts.) |
| **Cash flow statement** | Is real cash actually coming in? (Profit on paper isn't always cash.) |

You don't need to read these like an accountant. A beginner mostly wants to see a few healthy signs: **sales growing** year after year, **steady profit**, **not too much debt**, and a business you can actually understand.

> [!TIP] Buffett's rule of thumb
>
> The famous investor Warren Buffett only buys businesses he understands. If you can't explain in one sentence how a company makes money, that's a fine reason to skip it.

## 3. A few simple numbers to know {#ratios}

To compare companies, people use simple "ratios" — just one number divided by another. Three friendly ones:

| Ratio | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| **P/E ratio** (Price ÷ Earnings) | How expensive a share is compared to the profit it makes. A very high P/E means people expect big growth (and you're paying a premium). Best used to compare similar companies. |
| **Debt-to-equity** | How much loan the company carries versus its own money. Lower is generally safer. |
| **ROE** (Return on Equity) | How well the company turns owners' money into profit. Higher and steady is good. |

> [!NOTE] Don't over-think it
>
> These are just clues, never magic numbers. A "good" P/E for one industry is "bad" for another. Use ratios to ask better questions, not to get a final answer.

## 4. Technical analysis — reading the chart {#technical}

Technical analysis mostly ignores the business and instead studies the **price chart** — the patterns of how the price has moved. The belief is that the chart already reflects everyone's hopes and fears, and that patterns tend to repeat.

A few words you might hear:

- **Trend** — the general direction: up, down, or sideways. "The trend is your friend" is a popular saying.
- **Support** — a price level where buyers tend to step in and stop the fall (a "floor").
- **Resistance** — a price level where sellers tend to appear and cap the rise (a "ceiling").
- **Candlestick** — the little red/green bars on a chart that show each day's price range.

Technical analysis is mostly used by **traders** who buy and sell quickly (days or even minutes). It is a deep, tricky skill, and frankly not where a beginner should start.

## 5. So which should you use? {#which}

Here's the honest, beginner-friendly answer:

|  | Fundamental | Technical |
|---|---|---|
| **Question it answers** | Is this a good business to own? | Which way is the price moving now? |
| **Best for** | Long-term investing (years) | Short-term trading (days) |
| **Beginner-friendly?** | Yes ✅ | Not really ⚠️ |

> [!TIP] The beginner's path
>
> For most people — and almost certainly to begin with — **long-term investing in good businesses (or simply in index funds, see Module 3)** beats fast trading. It needs less skill, less screen-time, and less stress, and history has been kind to patient investors. You can always learn the rest later.
