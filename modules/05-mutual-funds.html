Module 5 · Mutual Funds (deep dive)

A mutual fund lets you pool your money with thousands of other investors so a professional can invest it for you — making it one of the easiest ways for a beginner in India to start.

1. What a mutual fund is

A mutual fund is a big shared pot of money. Many investors — maybe lakhs of people like you — put in small amounts, and that combined money is handled by a fund manager, a professional whose full-time job is to invest it wisely in things like company shares or bonds.

In return for your money, you receive units of the fund. If you invest ₹5,000 and each unit costs ₹50, you own 100 units. As the value of the fund's investments rises or falls, the value of your units does too.

  • Why it suits beginners: you don't need to pick individual stocks or watch the market all day — the manager does the hard work.
  • Diversification: even a small amount is spread across dozens of companies, so one bad stock won't sink you.
  • Run by AMCs: an AMC (Asset Management Company) is the firm that creates and manages funds. Eg: SBI Mutual Fund, HDFC Mutual Fund, ICICI Prudential, Axis.

REGULATED & SAFE TO USE

Every mutual fund in India is regulated by SEBI (the Securities and Exchange Board of India). SEBI sets strict rules on how your money is held and reported, so AMCs cannot simply run off with it. The market risk is real, but the structure is trustworthy.

2. Types of funds

Funds are grouped by what they invest in. Here are the main kinds a beginner will meet, with one plain line each.

Type of fundWhat it does
EquityInvests mainly in company shares — higher risk, but the best engine for long-term growth.
DebtInvests in bonds and fixed-income — steadier and safer, with lower returns.
HybridA mix of equity and debt in one fund — a middle path between growth and safety.
IndexSimply copies an index like the Nifty 50 or Sensex — very low cost, no star manager needed.
ELSSA tax-saving equity fund with a 3-year lock-in (you can't withdraw before that).
LiquidParks spare cash safely for a short time — very low risk, easy to take out.

START SIMPLE

For a brand-new investor with a long time horizon, a plain index fund is one of the easiest and cheapest places to begin. You don't have to understand the others on day one.

3. NAV (Net Asset Value)

The NAV, or Net Asset Value, is the price of one unit of the fund. It is worked out very simply:

  • NAV = total value of everything the fund holds ÷ total number of units.
  • It is calculated once a day, after markets close — not second-by-second like a stock price.

So when you invest today, you get that evening's NAV, not the price at the exact minute you clicked "buy".

MYTH TO BUST

A low NAV is not "cheaper" or better than a high NAV. A fund with NAV ₹15 is not a bargain over one with NAV ₹300. Eg: ₹6,000 buys 400 units of the first or 20 units of the second — but if both grow 10%, you earn the same ₹600. What matters is how well the fund grows, not the NAV number.

4. Direct vs Regular plans

Every fund comes in two versions, and choosing the right one quietly affects how much money you keep.

  • Regular plan: you buy through a distributor or agent, and the fund pays them a commission out of your money every year. This makes the fund's yearly fee (the expense ratio) higher.
  • Direct plan: you buy straight from the AMC with no middleman, so there's no commission — the fee is lower and more of the return stays with you.

The two plans hold the exact same investments. The only difference is cost. And because that small yearly gap keeps compounding, over 15–20 years it can quietly add up to a large sum.

RULE OF THUMB

If you are choosing the fund yourself, prefer the Direct plan. Same fund, lower cost, more in your pocket.

5. Costs: expense ratio & exit load

Funds aren't free to run, so they charge small fees. Knowing them helps you avoid paying more than you need to.

  • Expense ratio: a yearly fee charged as a percentage of your invested money. It's deducted automatically — you never get a bill. Eg: an index fund may charge around 0.2% a year, while many actively managed funds charge 1.5% or more.
  • Exit load: a small one-time fee for pulling your money out too early (often within a year). It exists to discourage hasty withdrawals. Eg: 1% if you redeem before 12 months.

These numbers look tiny, but they come off your returns every single year. A lower expense ratio quietly boosts what you keep over the long run — which is a big reason index funds are so attractive.

6. SIP vs lumpsum

This is simply about how you put money in.

  • SIP (Systematic Investment Plan): a fixed amount invested automatically every month. Eg: ₹5,000 on the 1st of each month, set once and forgotten.
  • Lumpsum: investing one big amount in a single go, Eg: ₹1,00,000 from a bonus all at once.

The quiet magic of a SIP is rupee-cost averaging: because the price moves, your fixed ₹5,000 buys more units when the market is cheap and fewer when it's expensive. Over time this smooths out your average buying price, so you don't need to guess the "right" moment.

BEST FOR MOST BEGINNERS

If you earn a monthly salary, a SIP usually suits you best — it builds a steady investing habit, matches your pay cycle, and removes the stress of timing the market.

7. How to pick a fund

You don't need to be an expert. Run through this simple checklist:

  1. Match it to your goal and time horizon. Money you need in 1–2 years should not go into an equity fund; money for a goal 10+ years away can.
  2. Default to a low-cost index fund. For a beginner, this is a perfectly fine and sensible starting choice.
  3. Check a long track record. Look at 5–10 year performance, not just last year's flashy number.
  4. Choose a reputable AMC. Stick with well-known, established fund houses.
  5. Never chase last year's winner. The top fund of one year is often an ordinary performer the next.

IMPORTANT

Past performance does not guarantee future returns. A great history is a hint, not a promise — treat it as one input, never the whole decision.

8. Common mistakes

AVOID THESE

A few errors trip up almost every new investor:

  • Chasing last year's top fund — yesterday's winner rarely repeats.
  • Stopping a SIP when markets fall — this is the worst time to stop, because your money is buying units cheaply.
  • Owning too many overlapping funds — five funds holding the same stocks is not real diversification, just clutter.
  • Judging a fund by its NAV — remember, a low NAV is not a discount.

THE SIMPLE STARTING POINT

For most beginners, a low-cost index fund bought through a monthly SIP and then left alone for years is a perfectly sensible way to begin. You don't need anything fancier to get started — patience does most of the work.
