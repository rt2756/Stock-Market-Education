1. Why invest at all?

Imagine you keep ₹1,00,000 in a cupboard at home. Ten years later it's still ₹1,00,000 — but it buys much less, because prices of everything (milk, petrol, rent) keep rising. That slow rise in prices is called inflation, and it quietly eats the value of money that just sits idle.

So the goal isn't only to save money — it's to make money grow faster than inflation. A savings account barely keeps up. Historically, owning small pieces of good businesses (shares) has been one of the better ways to grow money over many years.

The big idea

You work for money today. Investing is about making your money work for you, so that one day it can earn even while you sleep.

2. What is a share?

A share (also called a stock or equity) is a tiny piece of ownership in a company.

Think of a company as a large pizza cut into, say, 1,00,00,000 slices. If you own 100 slices, you own a (very small!) part of that company. If the company does well and becomes more valuable, your slices become more valuable too. If it does badly, they become less valuable.

When you own shares of a company, two nice things can happen:

  • The price can rise. You bought at ₹100, later it's worth ₹150 — that ₹50 gain is called capital appreciation.
  • You may get a share of the profit. Some companies pay out a slice of their yearly profit to owners. That payment is a dividend (more on this later).

The flip side: there's no guarantee. Prices can fall, and a company can do poorly. That's the risk you take in exchange for the chance of higher growth.

3. Where are shares traded? (NSE & BSE)

You don't buy shares directly from a company's office. They're bought and sold in a big organised marketplace called a stock exchange — a little like a vegetable mandi, but for company shares, and fully electronic.

India has two main exchanges:

  • NSE — National Stock Exchange
  • BSE — Bombay Stock Exchange (Asia's oldest, started 1875)

The exchange is just the meeting place that matches buyers with sellers and makes sure the trade happens fairly. Indian markets are open Monday to Friday, 9:15 AM to 3:30 PM (closed on weekends and market holidays).

4. Who keeps it fair? (SEBI & the participants)

For people to trust the market, someone has to make sure nobody cheats. In India that referee is SEBI — the Securities and Exchange Board of India. SEBI makes the rules, watches over the exchanges, brokers and companies, and protects ordinary investors like you.

The main people and bodies in the market:

WhoWhat they do
SEBIThe regulator (referee) that makes rules and protects investors.
Stock exchange (NSE/BSE)The marketplace that matches buyers and sellers.
Listed companiesBusinesses whose shares can be bought and sold.
BrokerThe licensed middleman who places your buy/sell orders on the exchange.
Depository (NSDL / CDSL)The "digital locker" system that holds everyone's shares safely in electronic form.
InvestorsPeople like you — and big players like mutual funds and banks.

5. The accounts you need

To buy shares, you need three things linked together. Good news: a broker sets all of this up for you in one go.

AccountThink of it as…
Bank accountYour wallet — where your money lives.
Trading accountThe remote control — you use it to place buy and sell orders.
Demat accountThe locker — it holds the shares you own, electronically. ("Demat" = dematerialised, i.e. no paper.)

When you buy, money leaves your bank account and shares appear in your Demat account. When you sell, shares leave the Demat account and money comes back to your bank. A broker (for example Zerodha, Groww, ICICI Direct, and many others) ties these together through an app or website. Once it's set up, Using Zerodha (Kite) walks through what the app's screens — the order window, Positions and Holdings — actually mean.

6. How a company gets listed (the IPO)

A company doesn't start out on the stock market. Usually it grows for years using the founders' money and investors, and one day decides it wants to raise a large amount of money from the public. To do that, it sells shares to people for the first time. This first sale is called an IPO — Initial Public Offering.

  • Primary market: the IPO itself — you buy new shares directly from the company.
  • Secondary market: everyday trading after listing — you buy shares from other investors, not the company. This is where 99% of activity happens.

In plain words

An IPO is a company's "we're open to the public now" moment. After that, its shares trade freely on the exchange every working day.

7. What makes a price move?

Share prices change every second during market hours. Why? In one phrase: supply and demand.

If more people want to buy a share than sell it, the price rises. If more people want to sell than buy, the price falls. That's it at heart — it's an auction happening live.

What makes people suddenly want to buy or sell? Things like:

  • The company's results — did it earn more profit than expected, or less?
  • News — a new product, a big order, a scandal, a change of boss.
  • The economy — interest rates, inflation, the government budget, global events.
  • Mood — plain old hope and fear. Markets are emotional in the short term.

In the short term, prices can swing on emotion and noise. Over the long term, a share tends to follow how well the actual business does. This is why patience matters so much.

8. Sensex & Nifty — the market's thermometer

You'll often hear "the market went up today." But there are thousands of companies — how do we measure "the market" in one number? With an index.

An index tracks a basket of important companies and turns their combined movement into a single number:

  • Nifty 50 — tracks 50 of the largest companies on the NSE.
  • Sensex — tracks 30 of the largest companies on the BSE.

When you hear "Nifty is up 1% today," it means this basket of big companies rose about 1% on average. An index is like a thermometer for the market's overall health — handy for seeing the big picture at a glance.

9. Dividends, bonus & splits

Sometimes a company does something that affects its shareholders directly. These are called corporate actions. The common ones:

ActionWhat it means
DividendThe company shares part of its profit with owners, as cash. Eg: ₹5 per share — own 100 shares, receive ₹500 in your bank.
Bonus sharesFree extra shares given to existing owners. Eg: a "1:1 bonus" doubles your share count (the price per share adjusts down, so total value is the same — just more slices).
Stock splitOne share is split into smaller ones to make the price more affordable. Eg: one ₹1,000 share becomes ten ₹100 shares. Same total value.
BuybackThe company buys back some of its own shares from the market, often a sign it feels the shares are cheap.

10. How to actually get started

You don't need to do any of this today — but here's what the path looks like, so it feels less mysterious:

  1. Keep your documents ready — PAN card, Aadhaar, a bank account, and a photo. These are needed for KYC (the identity check every investor must complete).
  2. Pick a broker and open an account online. It's mostly paperless and takes a day or two to activate. The trading + Demat accounts are created together.
  3. Add a small amount of money from your bank into the trading account.
  4. Start tiny. Buy one share of a company you know, just to see how it works. Watching ₹500 move teaches more than reading ten articles.
  5. Learn before you grow. Once it feels familiar, read Modules 2–4 before putting in more.

Go slow

There is no rush and no "missing out." The market will be here for decades. It's completely fine to spend weeks just reading and watching prices before buying anything. Starting small and slow is the smart move, not the timid one.

11. Glossary — words you'll keep hearing

WordSimple meaning
Share / Stock / EquityA small piece of ownership in a company.
BrokerThe licensed middleman (app/website) you trade through.
Demat accountThe electronic locker that holds your shares.
PortfolioThe full collection of investments you own.
Bull marketA period when prices are generally rising (optimism). 🐂
Bear marketA period when prices are generally falling (pessimism). 🐻
DividendCash paid by a company to its shareholders from profit.
Market capitalisation ("market cap")The total value of a company = share price × number of shares. Big companies = "large-cap"; smaller ones = "mid-cap" / "small-cap".
VolumeHow many shares were traded — high volume means lots of activity.
IndexA single number tracking a basket of major stocks (e.g. Nifty, Sensex).
VolatilityHow much and how fast a price jumps around. High volatility = bumpy ride.
LiquidityHow easily you can buy or sell something without moving its price much.
